      Why these factors

      Each scoring factor is tied to research or to how other cities rank bike projects. The research supports including each factor. It does not give exact weights: the default points copy what transportation agencies use (Safety ≈35, Equity ≈20, Connectivity ≈20–25, Demand the rest). Treat the weights as a starting position to discuss, not a finding.

      Street design, parking impact and ease

      • Street measurements come from IDOT's road inventory (2026 edition): curb-to-curb width, through travel lanes, parking on each side, daily traffic (mostly 2022–23 counts), speed limit and road owner. It covers essentially every arterial in the wards (IDOT road network service; field definitions in the IDOT roadway information manual, including surface width and parking).
      • Likely parking impact asks whether protected lanes on both sides fit within the measured width with today's lanes and parking. If not, it tries a road diet (four lanes to three, only where traffic is under 20,000 vehicles a day, roughly the range where the federal Road Diet guide reports most projects), then removing parking on one side, then both. Results depend on assumed widths; switch between standard and minimum in the Priorities tab to see the range (FHWA Road Diet Informational Guide).
      • Ease is a 0–100 difficulty score: parking impact 40%, traffic volume 25% (over 20,000 vehicles a day is hardest), road owner 25% (state roads need IDOT approval; county roads, county approval), and speed limit 10% (35 mph or more). Up to 33 is "Easier", up to 60 "Moderate", above that "Harder". These weights are a judgment call, not from a published method.
      • Limits. IDOT's records can lag recent changes: it still lists parking on both sides of several streets that already have protected lanes. The check ignores bus stops, loading zones, turn lanes and drainage, and it cannot tell how full the parking is. Treat the label as a screen; confirm with CDOT designs or a site visit before using it with businesses or neighbors. Parking occupancy counts are the standard way to show whether a block has spare parking.
      • Major streets with no bike facility are city arterials (city street centerline classes 2 and 3) with no bike route along them, split at crossing arterials at least a quarter mile apart. They are scored on the same factors as existing routes.

      Cost and budget assumptions (Budget plan)

      • Concrete-curb protected lane, $400K per mile. Chicago's 2022 program to add concrete curbs cost about $7 million for 25 miles of new and upgraded protected lanes, roughly $280K a mile in 2022 dollars (Chicago Sun-Times, 2022; CDOT). A cast-in-place curb lane on 31st St cost about $400K for half a mile in 2016, near $1M a mile today (Chicago Reader). $400K sits between the two, closer to the precast curbs CDOT now uses.
      • Flexible-post protected lane $300K, buffered lane $150K, neighborhood greenway $200K per mile. Based on 2025 planning estimates of $290K (posts), $110–140K (buffered) and $200–285K (greenways with traffic circles) per mile, both directions (Longmont, CO bike plan, App. E). Chicago greenways use fewer traffic circles, so the default is at the low end.
      • Painted lane, $125K per mile. National median $89K per mile in 2012 dollars, adjusted for inflation (Bushell et al., UNC Highway Safety Research Center for FHWA, 2013).
      • Contraflow lane, $150K per mile. No published figure found; estimated as paint plus signs.
      • Annual budget, default. About a year as the wards' population share (about ) of CDOT's $17M, two-year citywide bike program (2021–22), plus $250K from each ward's $1.5M yearly ward infrastructure fund ("menu money"; 40th Ward, 46th Ward). Menu money also pays for street, alley and sidewalk repair, so the real bike share depends on each alderperson.

      What agencies also weigh that this tool can't

      • Community support. Berkeley gives it 30 of 100 points. Ward offices are the right source for this.
      • Feasibility and cost. Parking removal, street width, bus routes and drainage decide what can be built. Not in this data.
      • Measured traffic volume and speed. NACTO's protected-lane triggers are 25+ mph, ~6,000+ vehicles per day, or more than one lane each way. This tool uses street type as a stand-in. IDOT's 2025 traffic counts exist but could not be loaded here.
      • Schools and intersections. Berkeley scores proximity to schools; the traffic-stress research stresses that dangerous crossings break networks. Neither is scored here.

      Context: evidence not used as a factor

      • Effects on businesses. A review of 23 U.S. and Canadian studies found bike and pedestrian projects have "generally positive or non-significant" effects on nearby retail and food businesses; car-dependent businesses can be hurt. Volker & Handy, Transport Reviews, 2021. Business density is scored as a destination measure, not as an economic-benefit claim.
      • Citywide safety. In 12 large U.S. cities over 13 years, more separated and protected lanes were associated with fewer road deaths for all users. Association, not proof of cause. Marshall & Ferenchak, Journal of Transport & Health, 2019 (summary).
      • Who the network is for. Most adults in U.S. cities are "interested but concerned" riders who would bike more on slower, quieter streets or separated paths. Dill & McNeil, Transportation Research Record 2587, 2016.

      North Side Bike Network · Wards

      The Case for Connectivity

      Our lives and daily activities - from work commutes and daycare drop-offs to visits with loved ones - don’t stop at the edges of ward boundaries, so why should our infrastructure? A complete bike grid would make all road users, including pedestrians and drivers, safer. This mapping tool shows how starting with minor improvements to connect our existing bike infrastructure across a cluster of wards could create a safer, continuous set of protected roads for everyone.

      What's already here

      A strong start

      Four kinds of bikeway already give riders real separation from fast traffic:

      • of protected lanes, with a curb or posts between bikes and cars
      • of contraflow lanes, letting bikes ride both ways on quiet one-way streets
      • of neighborhood greenways, side streets signed and calmed for biking
      • of off-street trails, like the lakefront and river trails

      Joined together, they already form one low-stress route network long.

      Where it breaks down

      Paint is not protection

      • of painted lanes
      • of buffered lanes (paint with a striped gap)
      • of shared-lane markings, arrows painted in traffic

      None of these put anything between a rider and a car. In a Vancouver and Toronto study, painted lanes on major streets showed no clear safety benefit, while the few protected cycle tracks studied had about one-ninth the injury risk of busy streets with no bike facility.

      The protected lanes that do exist come in separate pieces; the longest runs just . And of major streets, including Western Avenue, Irving Park Road and Peterson Avenue, have no bike facility at all (dotted on the map).

      Priorities

      Where to start

      These deaths are not inevitable. Stronger policy and safer street design make a real difference, and this tool is built to help decide where to invest next.

      The tool ranks every stretch where riders have no physical protection: painted lanes, buffered lanes, shared-lane markings, and major streets with no bike facility at all. Painted routes are already designated for biking, so adding a curb or posts is often the fastest way to stitch protected pieces together. Streets with nothing, like Irving Park, may need the most help but are often harder to change.

      Each segment gets a 0–100 score. Defaults follow how transportation agencies weigh bike projects:

      Safety 35Connects 25Equity 20Demand 20

      Safety: injuries per mile and how busy the street is. Connects: whether it links separate low-stress pieces. Equity: households without a car and residents in poverty nearby. Demand: businesses, density, L stations and current bike commuting. Sliders in the Priorities tab let you change every weight.

      The tool also rates how hard each fix is likely to be, using IDOT's street measurements: whether parking or a travel lane would have to go, how much traffic the street carries, its speed limit, and whether the state controls it. That separates quick wins from long-term campaigns.
